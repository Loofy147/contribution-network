# 1. Business Model — Contribution Network Bounties

**Status:** Working hypothesis, v0.1  
**Date:** 2026-10-12

## 1.1 Core thesis

The Contribution Network organizes project-based contributions of many forms into outcomes that matter to a customer, community, or beneficiary.

It combines:
- a project and outcome registry;
- a flexible contribution framework;
- discovery and coordination across professional and non-professional niches;
- verification and attribution;
- reward agreements and settlement records; and
- a commercial layer that funds operation of the network.

**Core distinction:** the project is the unit of coordination, each contribution is a unit of participation, and an accepted outcome is the unit of delivery.

The platform should not require every contribution to be a task, every value to have a monetary price, or every project to be commercial. It must, however, identify the applicable rules before participants take on obligations.

## 1.2 The actors

A single person or organization may play multiple roles, but the roles should be explicit.

| Actor | Need or role | Typical value received or provided |
|---|---|---|
| Project owner / outcome buyer | Wants a result delivered | Provides objective, constraints, acceptance criteria, and usually payment |
| Funder / sponsor | Wants a project or outcome supported | Provides money, resources, or conditional funding |
| Contributor | Offers a useful contribution | Receives agreed compensation, recognition, access, learning, impact, or another explicitly agreed benefit |
| Project steward / coordinator | Connects contributions and manages dependencies | May be paid by the project or network |
| Reviewer / verifier | Checks evidence and acceptance criteria | May receive a review bounty or service fee |
| End user / beneficiary | Uses or benefits from the outcome | May be a customer, community, public, or project partner |
| Network operator | Maintains infrastructure, trust, governance, and business operations | Earns disclosed platform revenue |

Do not treat beneficiaries as payers unless evidence shows they are willing and able to pay. Do not assume every contributor wants the same kind of compensation.

## 1.3 What the network provides

### For project owners
- A way to define an outcome without having to recruit every specialty independently.
- A structured way to request work, knowledge, assets, resources, feedback, funding, or distribution.
- Visibility into dependencies, evidence, contribution status, costs, risks, and delivery.
- Flexible participation: task bounty, milestone work, negotiated contribution, voluntary support, or a defined shared pool.
- A traceable handoff that clarifies what was delivered, by whom, under which terms, and with what limitations.

### For contributors
- Access to projects that may need their particular form of value, not only their job title.
- Clear expectations, acceptance criteria, deadlines, compensation and rights before committing.
- Recognition and evidence of accepted contributions.
- Multiple reward modes, without implying that all forms of value are directly comparable.
- A fair process for disagreement, revisions, attribution, and settlement.

### For funders and sponsors
- A transparent project objective and budget.
- Reporting on how money and other resources were used.
- Evidence of progress and accepted outputs.
- A separation between funding granted, actual money paid, estimated contribution value, and platform revenue.

## 1.4 What makes the model distinct

A simple bounty board lists tasks and pays for accepted submissions. This network aims to coordinate a broader project containing interdependent contributions from multiple niches.

Its potential differentiators are hypotheses to test:
1. A common contribution model across heterogeneous domains.
2. Project-level coordination and dependency management.
3. Clear separation of contribution evidence, value assessment, compensation and ownership.
4. Support for contributions with predefined, negotiated, optional, non-monetary, or outcome-dependent valuation/compensation arrangements.
5. Reusable evidence about completed outcomes and contributor reliability.
6. An operational mode that can range from self-service to managed delivery.

Neither “all niches” nor “whole-project delivery” alone proves differentiation. Adjacent offerings already cover crowdsourced challenges, broad freelance work, open-source sponsorship, and managed crowdsourced delivery. The differentiated value must be demonstrated through workflow, customer results, trust, or economics.

## 1.5 Operating modes

These are different business models, not merely UI settings. Keep all three as strategic options, but launch with only one. The current launch hypothesis is Mode B, delivered manually; the first pilots must clarify whether customers pay for coordination and whether the network can deliver it economically.

### Mode A — Self-service project
The project owner defines the project, accepts contributions, integrates work and is accountable for the outcome. The network supplies workflow, discovery, records and tools.

- Lower operational burden for the network.
- Customer retains more integration responsibility.
- Revenue may come from project tools, subscriptions or transaction fees.

### Mode B — Coordinated project
The network helps define work packages, source contributors, manage dependencies, review evidence, and track delivery. Accountability is split contractually.

- More support and greater control over coordination.
- Requires project stewardship and reliable review capacity.
- Potentially suitable for the first manual pilot.

### Mode C — Managed outcome
The network contracts to deliver a defined result, coordinates contributors, integrates outputs and accepts more commercial responsibility.

- Stronger customer experience and clearer outcome-level value proposition.
- Higher execution, rework, refund and quality risk.
- Requires pricing sufficient to cover integration, QA, risk reserve and customer support.

The contract must identify who is responsible for scope, decisions, acceptance, revisions and final delivery.

## 1.6 Project and funding models

The system should support several project origins without forcing one financial structure:
- customer-funded project;
- sponsor-funded project;
- community-funded pool;
- grant or nonprofit-funded project;
- internally funded project;
- project whose future revenue may support maintenance or agreed rewards.

For any project, distinguish:
- committed budget;
- funding received and available;
- reserved contributor rewards;
- paid rewards;
- outstanding liabilities;
- project operating costs;
- network fees;
- unspent or refundable funds.

An unfunded public idea may be listed as an opportunity, but it must not be represented as a funded bounty. In-kind support must be recorded separately from cash.

## 1.7 Revenue model

Potential streams, in likely order of exploration:

1. **Project coordination fee:** charge for breaking down and coordinating contributions.
2. **Managed delivery margin:** price an outcome and pay contributors/direct delivery costs from project funds.
3. **Transaction fee:** disclose a fee on eligible monetized transactions or payments.
4. **Business subscription:** recurring access to team workflows, reporting, private projects, integrations and governance tools.
5. **Verification/review services:** charge for independent checks, domain review, moderation or evidence packs.
6. **Enterprise tools and integrations:** later, once repeat workflows exist.
7. **Asset licensing or revenue participation:** only where ownership, license, permissions, calculation and distribution terms are contractually clear.

These are candidate revenue models, not validated revenue sources. Do not count the entire project budget or the estimated value of non-cash contributions as network revenue.

## 1.8 Initial recommendation

Start as a **human-led, buyer-funded coordination service** for one narrow, repeatable outcome that requires multiple materially different contributions. A named project steward helps define the work, source contributions, coordinate dependencies, manage review, and record the accepted result.

The customer and agreement must state who is accountable for integration and final acceptance. Do not imply that the network guarantees the final result unless the service is explicitly priced and contracted to assume that risk.

The first offer is not a public all-purpose marketplace, not a self-service SaaS product, and not a guarantee of managed delivery. It is a paid manual service using a broad contribution framework internally. Keep the underlying model extensible; keep the launch promise narrow.

Choose the first use case based on recent buyer pain, an identifiable budget and decision-maker, observable acceptance criteria, a real need to combine contribution types, a manual supply path, manageable legal/data risk, and a credible path to repeat demand. Do not select a niche solely because it is familiar or exciting.

Before custom software, use a manual pilot and measure the full cost of coordination, review, integration, rework, acquisition, and settlement. A provisional default is three paid pilots in the same use case across at least two customers before committing to a broader build; document any exception and the evidence supporting it.

Keep all other modes and revenue streams as hypotheses. Do not choose a token model, universal value score, fixed commission rate, complex royalty system or cooperative governance structure until research and pilots give a specific reason to do so.

## 1.9 Core assumptions to falsify

- Customers prefer an integrated outcome to buying each contribution independently.
- Contributors will participate when terms, reward options, attribution, and dispute paths are clear.
- The network can assess heterogeneous contributions without inventing false precision.
- Coordination and verification improve delivery enough to justify their costs.
- A repeatable project category can generate revenue above direct contributor, review, coordination and payment costs.
- Project-level reputation and evidence improve future matching without unfairly excluding new contributors.
