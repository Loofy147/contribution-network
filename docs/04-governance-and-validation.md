# 4. Governance, Trust and Market Validation

**Status:** Working framework, v0.1  
**Date:** 2026-10-12

## 4.1 Governance principles

The network must make it clear:
- who owns the project and can make decisions;
- who funds a request and whether funds are available;
- who can contribute and what is ineligible;
- how contributions are evaluated and who reviews them;
- how rewards and expenses are agreed and settled;
- who owns or licenses submitted materials;
- how conflicts, rejections, revisions and appeals are handled;
- what information is public, private or confidential;
- who is accountable for the integrated outcome.

Project-specific terms may vary, but the records and process should be consistent.

## 4.2 Fair review and disputes

Before work starts, publish criteria and identify the decision-maker. For an accepted/rejected contribution:
- preserve the submission and decision evidence;
- provide a reason tied to the published criteria;
- distinguish correction of a defect from scope expansion;
- define number/scope of included revisions when relevant;
- give a route to challenge material errors or conflicts of interest;
- record the resolution and any settlement effect.

For high-value or disputed work, use a second reviewer or independent check. Where practical, the original author should not be the only judge of their own work.

Do not promise that every dispute can be solved by an automated score or vote.

## 4.3 Trust, abuse and integrity risks

Anticipate:
- spam or low-quality submissions;
- duplicate or plagiarized contributions;
- fabricated credentials, metrics or evidence;
- unlicensed or stolen assets;
- poisoned, sensitive or unlawfully obtained data;
- collusion, fake reviews and conflicts of interest;
- contributor manipulation of a reward pool;
- project owners changing scope after work;
- non-payment after acceptance;
- false claims that a check was run or a result is guaranteed.

Mitigations include relevant identity/eligibility checks, provenance records, explicit acceptance tests, limited permissions, reproducible evidence, reviewer separation, rate limits where suitable, audit history, transparent terms, appeal paths and a way to report abuse.

Do not require excessive identity data from all participants by default; select controls proportional to risk and applicable law.

## 4.4 Intellectual property, privacy and compliance

Before launching, obtain jurisdiction-specific advice on:
- contributor and customer contracts;
- IP assignment, licensing, attribution and third-party assets;
- privacy, confidential data, data retention and deletion;
- worker classification and local employment rules;
- tax, invoicing and cross-border payouts;
- payment services, escrow and handling of client funds;
- sanctions, fraud and prohibited project activity;
- consumer protection and refund policies, where relevant.

A public repository or publicly posted contribution does not automatically grant rights to reuse it. A voluntary contribution is not automatically an assignment of ownership. A platform fee or pooled reward is not inherently an escrow arrangement.

Do not hold funds or promise escrow before an appropriate payment provider and legal arrangement are established.

## 4.5 Reputation

Start with evidence-based project records rather than a single universal contributor score. Separate:
- accepted contributions and their scope;
- domain-specific review outcomes;
- timeliness and communication;
- disputes and reversals;
- project complexity and context;
- verified customer feedback;
- roles such as reviewer, contributor, funder or steward.

Give participants ways to correct factual errors and challenge unfair decisions. Do not penalize someone merely for attempting a difficult task that was properly scoped, and do not equate paid volume or popularity with competence.

## 4.6 Market hypotheses to validate

The following are not yet established facts:
1. A customer will pay to coordinate a project through contributions from different niches instead of hiring separately.
2. A project-level outcome is a more compelling purchase than individual tasks for at least one repeatable customer segment.
3. A flexible value/reward framework helps attract contributors without increasing disputes or coordination costs excessively.
4. Project records, attribution and reusable reputation improve trust and repeat participation.
5. Network revenue can cover coordination, review, payments, acquisition, support and risk costs.

## 4.7 Customer discovery

Start with one candidate customer segment. Interview people who recently purchased or managed work requiring multiple forms of expertise or resources.

Ask about actual past behavior:
- What outcome were you trying to achieve?
- What steps/contributors were required?
- What was bought, funded, donated or provided in-kind?
- Where did coordination, verification, recruitment or integration fail?
- What did delays and rework cost?
- How did you decide whether each contribution was good enough?
- How were contributors paid or recognized?
- What did you do instead of using a platform?
- Who has authority and budget to buy a better process?
- What measurable result would justify paying for coordinated delivery?

Do not treat compliments, hypothetical interest or free sign-ups as proof of willingness to pay.

## 4.8 Competitive research

Compare adjacent categories, not only platforms called "bounty" sites:
- challenge and innovation platforms;
- freelance marketplaces;
- managed crowdsourced delivery;
- open-source sponsorship/grants;
- paid review and assurance services;
- community/cooperative platforms;
- asset/licensing markets and specialized talent networks.

For each competitor, record target buyer, project scope, contribution types, acceptance method, funding/reward arrangement, coordination responsibility, pricing/fees, IP terms, disputes, and evidence of delivered outcomes. Date-stamp pricing and terms; they change.

A claim that no direct competitor exists requires documented market mapping. Do not claim uniqueness from a small list of competitors.

## 4.9 Manual pilot

Run one real, scoped project without first building the full platform.

Pilot outline:
1. Secure a real project owner and a concrete outcome.
2. Agree acceptance criteria, budget, responsibilities, data/IP rights and rewards.
3. Create a contribution ledger using a spreadsheet or issue tracker.
4. Invite a small group with several different contribution types.
5. Track proposed, agreed, submitted, accepted, rejected, disputed and settled contributions.
6. Deliver and obtain the owner's acceptance.
7. Honor reward agreements.
8. Calculate actual economics and interview customer/contributors.
9. Decide whether to repeat, revise or stop.

For validation of commercial demand, prefer a paid pilot. If the first project is self-funded or voluntary, label the commercial gate incomplete.

## 4.10 Decision gates

Suggested gates for moving beyond a manual pilot (adjust once scope and pricing are known):
- real customer accepts a specified outcome;
- several meaningfully different contribution types are used;
- each accepted contribution has an attributable record and evidence;
- compensation and rights terms were understood and honored;
- delivery completes without unresolved material disputes;
- actual project economics identify a credible path to positive contribution margin;
- customer indicates credible repeat demand, ideally evidenced by a follow-on purchase or referral;
- contributors consider the process fair enough to participate again.

One successful project is learning, not proof that the model works across all niches. Repeat the process across a few projects before building a broad automated marketplace.

## 4.11 Legal and operating guardrail

This document is a business-model framework, not legal, tax or financial advice. The rules applicable to the network depend on the operator's jurisdiction, contributor and customer locations, how projects are structured, how money is handled, and the specific agreements used.
